**At a glance**

- **Company:** Mepo Indonesia (PT. Multi Kreasi Travel Indo), a travel platform
- **Path:** QA Engineer Intern (Dec 2025 – Mar 2026) → Product Manager Intern (Mar 2026 – Oct 2026)
- **Outcome:** Best Award at the end of the PM internship; PM competency scores of 8–9/10 across 23 criteria

## Context

Mepo is a travel platform operated by PT. Multi Kreasi Travel Indo. I joined in December 2025 as a Quality Assurance Engineer Intern, testing new and existing features before release. At the end of March 2026 I moved into the Product Manager Intern role at the same company, where I stayed until the end of October 2026.

This case study is about that transition: what changed when I went from checking whether something was built correctly to deciding what should be built, and how my QA work shaped the way I do product work.

## Problem

As a QA engineer I spent most of my time on the gap between what was asked for and what was delivered. I validated REST APIs in Swagger UI and Postman, ran regression tests on new builds, verified frontend slicing against product requirements, and logged defects in JIRA with reproduction steps and expected versus actual results.

Many of the issues I logged came down to the same question: *what exactly was this feature supposed to do?* When a requirement leaves room for interpretation, engineering and QA can each read it differently, and the difference only shows up late, during testing or UAT. As a PM intern, my problem was the mirror image of my QA work: write requirements clear enough that a tester could check them and an engineer could build them without guessing.

## Research & insight

My first source of insight was the testing work itself. Designing test cases and test scenarios for new features meant breaking each feature into concrete, checkable behaviours, including edge cases and error states. Running UAT meant checking features against business requirements, not only against technical specs.

As a PM intern, the assessment of my work covered product discovery and user research, requirement analysis, and research into business value and partner APIs, all within the context of an online travel business. The key insight I carried over from QA was simple: **a requirement is only finished when someone can test it.** If I could not write the acceptance criteria, the requirement was not ready for engineering yet.

## Decisions & prioritization

The main decisions in my PM role were about what goes into the backlog and in what order. My assessment covered prioritization and requirement analysis, Jira and product backlog management, and working in an Agile/Scrum development cycle.

Two choices shaped how I worked:

- **Define before building.** I would rather spend more time on specifications and acceptance criteria up front than discover gaps during testing. The trade-off is a slower start on each item, in exchange for fewer surprises at the end.
- **Use what QA knows.** Defects and test findings are evidence about where the product is fragile. I treated them as input for prioritization, not only as tickets to close.

## Solution

In practice, my work as a Product Manager Intern meant:

- Defining product requirements and translating user needs into clear specifications for the development team
- Writing PRDs, user stories, and acceptance criteria, and managing them in the Jira backlog
- Driving feature development through cross-functional collaboration with engineering and stakeholders
- Taking part in product testing, bug reporting, and UAT, so that what shipped matched what was specified

Because I had already worked with the same team as a QA engineer, including daily stand-ups and Agile workflows, I could talk to developers in terms of endpoints, response structures, and edge cases, which made specifications easier to discuss and agree on.

## Results & metrics

- **Best Award** at the end of the Product Manager internship, given in recognition of strategic thinking, a user-centric approach, and cross-functional collaboration
- **PM competency assessment:** scores between 8/10 and 9/10 across all 23 criteria. The top score of 9/10 was given for 9 criteria, including task and requirement understanding, quality and accuracy of work, problem solving and decision making, Jira and product backlog management, and product testing, bug reporting and UAT
- **QA competency assessment:** scores between 9/10 and 9.5/10, with 9.5/10 for test case design and for SDLC & STLC; the certificate recognises a key role in identifying issues and improving product reliability

## What I learned

1. **QA is a strong foundation for product work.** Thinking in test cases made my requirements more precise, and my PM scores were highest in the areas closest to that background: requirement understanding, backlog management, and testing and UAT.
2. **Data is my next growth area.** My lowest PM scores (8/10) were in data analysis and product metrics, travel and OTA business understanding, and business value and partner/API research. Next time I would set success metrics for each feature before development starts, so impact can be measured after release.
3. **The role changes, the team does not.** Moving from QA to PM inside the same company meant I already had trust and context with engineering. What I had to add was the product side: deciding what matters, not only whether it works.
